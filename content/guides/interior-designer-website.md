**The short answer:** a good interior design website does three things. It shows finished projects so well that visitors can imagine living in them, it makes clear what kind of client and budget you work with, and it makes getting in touch feel easy and personal. Most interior design sites get the first one half right and miss the other two.

Interior design is bought on trust and taste. Nobody hires a designer after one visit to a website, but almost everyone checks the website before they call. That visit decides whether you're on the shortlist, and whether the people who get in touch are the clients you actually want.

## What potential clients look for on an interior designer's website

When someone lands on your site, usually from Instagram, a referral or a Google search, they're quietly asking four questions:

1. **Is this my taste?** Do the finished rooms look like something I'd want to live or work in?
2. **Is this my budget?** Am I the kind of client this studio works with, or am I wasting their time?
3. **Can I trust them?** Have they done projects like mine, and are there real clients behind the photos?
4. **What happens if I get in touch?** Is it a form into the void, or a conversation with a person?

Every section of your site should answer at least one of these.

## The pages every interior design website needs

### 1. A home page that leads with the work

Your home page is a first impression, not a brochure. Open with your strongest photograph or a short, quiet video of a finished space, and one plain line saying what you do and where. For example: *"Residential and hospitality interiors in London and the Cotswolds."*

Skip the vague taglines like *"Designing spaces that inspire"*. They don't help visitors, and they don't help Google or AI assistants understand what you do.

### 2. Project pages, one per project

This is the most important part of the site, and where most studios fall short. A grid of thumbnails isn't enough. Each significant project deserves its own page with:

- **10 to 25 strong photographs,** with wide shots first, then details: joinery, materials, lighting, styling.
- **A short story:** who the client was, the brief, the main challenge and how you solved it. Three or four short paragraphs is plenty.
- **The facts:** type of project, location (city or region is enough), size, scope (full renovation, furnishing, styling), and year.
- **Before photos, where you have them.** Transformation is the most persuasive thing an interior designer can show.

Separate project pages also mean each one can appear in Google searches like *"modern apartment interior in Dubai Marina"* or *"boutique hotel interior designer"*.

### 3. Services and process

Clients, especially first-time ones, don't know how hiring an interior designer works. A clear process page removes the fear:

- what you offer (full-service design, design consultation, furnishing and styling, turnkey projects),
- the steps from first call to handover,
- roughly how long a typical project takes,
- what the client is expected to do and decide.

### 4. About, with real people

People hire a person, not a logo. Show the founder and team, with real photographs, a short story of how the studio started, and what you care about in a space. A few lines in your own voice beat a polished paragraph that could belong to anyone.

### 5. Contact that feels like a conversation

Make the next step obvious on every page, not only on the contact page. The best interior design sites offer more than one way in:

- a **short enquiry form** that asks the right questions (see below),
- **WhatsApp or a direct phone number** for clients who'd rather talk,
- **booking a call** for studios that work that way.

For [PKD Studio](/work/pkd-studio), an interior design practice in Mumbai, we presented projects as an editorial gallery and wired the site straight to WhatsApp, so a visitor who loves a room is one tap away from talking to the founder. Choose whichever channel your clients actually use.

## The details that separate good sites from great ones

### Photography is the whole game

No amount of design can rescue average photos of your work. Invest in a professional interiors photographer for your best projects, shoot in natural light, and style each space before the shoot. Don't use phone snapshots, renders presented as finished work, or stock images of rooms you didn't design.

Renders are fine for projects in progress, as long as they're clearly labelled as renders.

### Say who you work with, and roughly at what budget

This is the step most studios avoid, and it's the one that saves the most time. You don't need a price list, but a line like *"Our full-service projects typically start from [amount]"* or *"We work on homes of 2,000 sq ft and above"* filters out mismatched enquiries and reassures the clients you want.

If you'd rather not mention money, let the work and the wording do it: the projects you show, the language you use and the questions in your form all signal what level you work at.

### Ask better questions in your enquiry form

A form that asks only for name, email and message produces vague enquiries. Ask a few useful questions instead:

- type of project (home, office, hospitality, retail),
- location,
- approximate size,
- scope (full design, specific rooms, styling only),
- timeline,
- budget range, as optional choices rather than an empty box,
- how they found you.

Keep it to one screen. Every answer helps you reply faster and with something specific.

### Speed, especially on phones

Interior design sites are image-heavy, which makes them slow if they're built carelessly, and most visitors arrive on a phone from Instagram. Images should be properly compressed and sized, in modern formats like WebP or AVIF, and load as the visitor scrolls. A beautiful gallery that takes six seconds to appear loses people before they see your best room.

### Let the design step back

Your website is a frame for your work, not a second portfolio piece competing with it. Generous white space, restrained typography and a calm layout let the photography carry the page. Animation should be slow and subtle, like a gallery, not a showroom.

## Instagram is not a substitute for a website

Many interior designers get most of their attention on Instagram, and it's tempting to treat it as the whole online presence. It shouldn't be:

- **You don't own it.** The algorithm decides who sees your work, and an account can be restricted overnight.
- **It doesn't rank on Google.** Someone searching *"interior designer in Toronto"* will find websites and map listings, not your grid.
- **It's poor at depth.** A carousel can't hold a project's full story, process and details the way a project page can.

Use Instagram to get attention and your website to win trust and enquiries. Link to your site in your bio, and to specific project pages from posts.

## Getting found on Google and AI search

- **Name your location and services plainly** on the home page, the services page and the project pages.
- **Set up a Google Business Profile** with photos of finished projects and ask happy clients for reviews. For *"interior designer near me"* searches, this map listing appears before any website.
- **Give every project its own page** with a descriptive title, rather than one long gallery.
- **Add structured data** that tells search engines and AI tools you're an interior design business, where you are and what you do.
- **Make sure your text is in the page source,** because many AI assistants, like ChatGPT and Perplexity, don't run JavaScript when they read a site.

If your site isn't appearing at all, [why your website isn't showing up on Google](/guides/why-your-website-is-not-showing-on-google) walks through the common causes.

## Common mistakes on interior design websites

- **One giant gallery** with no project names, stories or context.
- **Text inside images,** which Google and AI tools can't read.
- **Autoplay music or long intro animations** before the visitor sees anything.
- **Outdated projects** at the top, or the newest work buried.
- **No clear next step**, so visitors admire the work and leave.
- **A template that looks like every other studio's.** If your work is distinctive, the site presenting it should be too.

## Template or custom website?

A template from Squarespace or Wix can work for a studio just starting out with a handful of projects. As your work and fees grow, the website starts to set expectations: a premium client comparing three studios notices when one site feels considered and the others feel off-the-shelf.

A custom website lets the design match your aesthetic, loads faster with large galleries, and is built around how your clients actually enquire. ## Questions

### What should an interior design website include?

An interior design website should include a home page that leads with your best work, a separate page for each significant project with photos, a short story and key facts, a services and process page, an about page with real people, and an easy way to get in touch, such as a short enquiry form, WhatsApp or a booking link. It should also signal what kind of client and budget you work with.

### How many projects should an interior designer show on their website?

Quality matters more than quantity. Six to twelve well-photographed projects, each with its own page, are more convincing than a gallery of forty. Show your best and most recent work first, and remove projects that no longer represent the level you work at.

### Should interior designers show prices on their website?

You don't need a price list, but giving a sense of budget helps. A line such as "full-service projects typically start from" a certain amount, or a minimum project size, filters out mismatched enquiries and reassures the clients you want. An optional budget range in the enquiry form works well too.

### Is Instagram enough for an interior designer?

No. Instagram is good for attention, but you don't own it, it doesn't rank on Google for searches like "interior designer near me", and it can't hold a project's full story. Use Instagram to attract people and a website to win their trust and enquiries.

### What is the best website platform for an interior designer?

For a studio just starting out, a template builder like Squarespace can be enough. For an established studio with premium clients, a custom-built website is usually better: it matches your aesthetic, loads large image galleries faster, gives full control over SEO and is built around how your clients enquire.

### How can an interior designer get more clients from their website?

Show each project in depth with professional photography, state clearly where you work and what you do, make contact easy from every page, ask useful questions in the enquiry form, set up a Google Business Profile with reviews, and make sure the site loads quickly on phones, since most visitors arrive from Instagram on mobile.
